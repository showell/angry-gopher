Learn to play Lyn Rummy

Lyn Rummy is a card game with a little luck, lots of fun, and an unexpected amount of skill. It works for one or more players: most games are two or three players taking turns, and larger groups work fine if you deal fewer cards per hand and bring a little patience. Solitaire works too; the pleasure there is closer to finishing a jigsaw puzzle than to winning a poker hand.

Each player holds a hand of cards, and all players play their cards to a shared board in the middle of the table. On your turn you try to play cards from your hand onto the board, rearranging what's already there as much as you like. The one requirement: before your turn ends, the board must be clean, meaning every stack on it is a legal meld. That shared, endlessly rearrangeable board is the heart of the game, and most of this page is about it.

You can play at a kitchen table with real cards, or right here at /game (the hosted game is the two-player version). It's the same game either way, and this page teaches it from scratch.

Quick start

Got a table, two decks, and some players? Here's the whole game:

  1. Shuffle the two decks together, setting any jokers aside (Lyn Rummy doesn't use them). Deal 12 to 15 cards to each player (dealer's choice, as long as everyone gets the same number), and stack the rest face-down in the middle. That's the draw pile.
  2. Arrange your hand to help you spot cards worth playing. Here is one traditional arrangement, by suit and descending: 
    A♠ K♠ 8♠ 5♠ Q♥ J♥ 7♥ K♣ 9♣ 4♣ 6♦ 2♦
    That's just one approach; any arrangement that helps you see melds forming is the right one.
  3. The player to the dealer's left goes first, and play moves clockwise. On your turn, lay any melds (see below) from your hand onto the table. Cards go straight from your hand to the table; there is no discard pile. Even a single meld is a fine turn. The one rule: when you end your turn, every stack on the table must be a legal meld.
  4. Once cards are on the table, players can also rearrange and build on them. More on that below.
  5. Draw at the end of your turn, based on how the turn went. If you played nothing from your hand (rearranging the board doesn't count), draw three cards. If you played your entire hand, draw five. Anything in between: no draw.
  6. The first player to play out their whole hand is the victor. That's a point of honor, not the end of the game: draw your five and keep playing until the draw pile is empty. There's no scorekeeping. Clever moves are their own reward, and everyone at the table can tell who's making them.

The three legal melds

Cards on the board sit in rows, each card laid side by side with its neighbors. Different players will call a row of cards a row, a meld, or a stack, or just say runs and sets, depending on the rummy games they've played before; this page says stack. A stack is a legal meld when it has three or more cards and forms one of three shapes. Each category below is laid out the way it might look mid-game on the kitchen table, several examples to a table.

Set — same value, all different suits

Three or four cards of the same value. No suit may repeat, so a set never grows past four cards: one per suit. 

at (24,20): A♠ A♥ A♦
at (31,90): 7♣ 7♦ 7♥ 7♠
at (19,162): Q♥ Q♠ Q♣
at (22,236): 10♠ 10♥ 10♦ 10♣

Pure run — consecutive values, one suit

Three or more consecutive values, all in a single suit. Runs may also turn the corner at the ace: the king is followed by the ace, and the ace by the two, so the last example below — J Q K A 2 — is a perfectly legal run. 

at (26,20): 5♣ 6♣ 7♣
at (18,90): 9♦ 10♦ J♦ Q♦
at (30,162): 3♥ 4♥ 5♥ 6♥ 7♥
at (21,236): J♠ Q♠ K♠ A♠ 2♠

Red/black run — consecutive values, alternating colors

Three or more consecutive values whose colors alternate red and black. Hearts and diamonds are the red suits; spades and clubs are the black ones. Which suit each card has doesn't matter, only its color. Red/black runs can turn the corner at the ace too, like the Q K A 2 3 example. 

at (22,20): 9♥ 10♠ J♦
at (33,90): 3♠ 4♥ 5♣ 6♦
at (17,162): 8♣ 9♦ 10♠ J♥ Q♣
at (26,236): Q♦ K♣ A♥ 2♠ 3♦

Here is where it gets fun

If you've played other rummy games, here is the twist that changes everything: melds on the board are never locked. The board is shared by all players, and on your turn you may rearrange it freely — split any stack, steal a card out of one meld to finish another, rebuild whole rows — as long as the board is clean again by the time your turn ends. Here's a board from the middle of a real game. (Lyn Rummy is played with two decks, so don't be surprised to spot the same card twice.)

at (260,343): T♥' J♥ Q♥ K♥
at (443,347): 2♥ 3♥ 4♥
at (19,92): A♥ A♦ A♣'

at (52,272): 8♠' 8♣' 8♥

Every stack up there is a legal meld right now, but nothing is frozen. Look at the long red/black run that starts with the A♠. That ace isn't stuck: the run reads 2 through 9 perfectly well without it, and the three-ace set near the top-left corner is already legal but has room for exactly one more ace: a spade. The board above is live, so try the move yourself: click the A♠ to break it off the run, then drag it over to the aces. Plays like this, melds feeding each other, are the heart of every Lyn Rummy turn.

One more thing every new player asks: what if you tear the board apart and can't put it back together? Mid-turn mess is completely legal: partial stacks, stray cards, half-finished ideas. You only have to leave the board clean. So if an experiment fails, put the cards back the way they were. The online game gives you a real Undo button; at the kitchen table, the undo button is your memory.
